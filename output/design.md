# Storefront design (Problem 10)

The goal: make Campus Customs feel like a contemporary collegiate apparel label, not an ecommerce template, while keeping the Yale identity and every existing feature (filters, chat, search grid, login, memory, product pages).

## Direction

- **Palette.** Yale Blue `#00356B` for brand fields (hero, header, patches). Yale's secondary blue `#286DC0` for anything you can click or that's active. Brand navy `#0A2240` for text. White surfaces, and a cool mist `#EDF1F6` behind every product photo. Felt-pennant yellow `#F2C230` is used sparingly, only for "only N left", stitching and text selection. No crimson anywhere, since that's Harvard.
- **Type.** Libre Caslon Display for big headlines (Yale's own identity is an old-style serif). Hanken Grotesk for reading and UI. Graduate, a varsity block face, appears only as varsity lettering on the chat patch, the size tiles, the size filters and the card hang-tags, like chenille letters on a jacket.
- **Removed template tells.** All-caps eyebrow labels above every heading, the one-italic-word headline, "A · B · C" middle-dot strings, "→" on links, and identical cards with the same drop shadow. The mist tone is cool on purpose, not the usual cream.

## What changed, and why it should help people stay and buy

1. **Products are rendered as clean cutouts** (`backend/cutouts.py`). The provided photos sit on mixed pure-black and pure-white backdrops, and some have black side bars, which showed up as boxes and stray black lines. The backend flood-fills each backdrop from the image edges, softens the edge, trims and centers every garment at the same scale, and caches a transparent WebP (`/media/cutouts/<name>.webp`). The originals are untouched, and the cache is gitignored. A quality check (the share of holes inside the garment's outline) keeps any photo whose shadows are as black as its backdrop as a framed photo instead of showing a broken cutout. 101 of 102 products are cutouts; the Basic Hoodie Big Yale stays a framed photo. *Why:* garments floating on one calm mist color make the catalogue look like one brand's lookbook instead of a pile of supplier photos, and nothing distracts from the product.
2. **Hero is a real clothing rack** (`Rack.tsx`). Twelve cutout garments hang directly on hangers from a chrome rail, dealt across categories. On load they slide along the rail one after another (the page's only automatic motion). Hovering makes a garment sway. The sway always finishes, so it never flickers or restarts. Dragging with a mouse scrolls the rack while the garments lean with your speed; on phones it's a native swipe. Previous/next arrows, a "Drag the rail or use the arrows" hint ("Swipe…" on touch) and a fade on whichever edge has more make the browsing obvious. Each garment has a paper swing-tag with name and price. *Why:* the first screen shows real merchandise with prices, browsing a rack is familiar from real stores, and every hanger is one click from its product page.
3. **Categories as a display window** (`CategoryList.tsx`). Big serif category rows sit on the left with live counts ("27 styles"). A shop-window stage on the right deals three real garments from whichever row you hover or tab to, fanned like cards with a small spring, plus a caption with the real count and starting price ("27 styles, from $32.00"). The active row gets a blue accent bar, and the text never moves or gets covered; an earlier version where a photo followed the cursor covered the words and lagged behind the row. On phones each row shows a garment thumbnail instead. *Why:* shoppers preview what a category actually holds, and what it costs, before committing to a click. Each garment in the window opens directly.
4. **Product cards show the sizes on the shelf.** Cutout garments float on mist at a 4:5 garment ratio and lift slightly on hover. On hover a hang-tag slides up listing XS–XXL in varsity letters, with sold-out sizes struck through (always visible on touch). "Only N left" badges in pennant yellow flag low stock. *Why:* shoppers learn whether their size exists before clicking, which cuts dead-end clicks, and real scarcity is a nudge to buy.
5. **Product detail page.** The photo magnifies 2× under the cursor so you can check the print and fabric. Size tiles use varsity lettering, with a stock bar under each (yellow when low, struck out and hatched when sold out). There's a clear "Pick a size" call to action, and a new **"Ask the chat about this"** button that opens the chat pre-filled with "Do you have this in M?". *Why:* confidence about fit and stock is what turns a look into a purchase, and the button puts help one tap away on the page where the decision happens.
6. **Navigation.** One underline slides between links, following the pointer and springing back to the current page. The header blurs over content and condenses on scroll, with the announcement bar folding away. The shield logo is a real heraldic shape. On phones the menu wipes open with large serif links. *Why:* the nav stays out of the way while scrolling, but it's always one tap away.
7. **Buttons and filters.** Buttons fill with a stripe that sweeps across on hover, and press down on click. The Products filter bar sticks under the header while you scroll results. Size chips use varsity lettering, color swatches pop when chosen, and active-filter chips animate in. On phones the filters fold behind one button so products appear on the first screen. *Why:* narrowing 102 products stays possible at any scroll position, without the filters crowding a phone screen.
8. **Chat styling.** The launcher is a stitched chenille "Y" patch labelled "Ask us". The panel springs open from the corner with a stitched seam under the header. Bubbles slide in, option buttons invert on hover, and the send button tilts. *Why:* a friendly, on-brand entry point gets people asking questions, and those questions lead to real product cards and sales.
9. **About page.** A big serif intro, the story set in serif at a comfortable reading measure, and an "On the shelf right now" panel with live category counts linking into the catalogue, in place of the old generic stat boxes. *Why:* every section of the story now leads back to shopping.

## Quality floor

- **Responsive:** checked at 1440px and 390px wide. The product grid drops to 2 columns on phones, and the rack becomes a swipe.
- **Accessible:** visible keyboard focus rings, `prefers-reduced-motion` turns off all animation, hover-only information is always visible on touch, and color contrast meets AA for text.
- **Everything still works:** the Problem 7 and 9 browser tests still pass (search grid, filters, chat loading and duplicate-send lock, clarification buttons, alternatives, login, saved history, product pages). The one "failure" was a Problem 7 test still expecting the old "102 products" wording from before Problem 9.
